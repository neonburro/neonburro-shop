# the shop, where it is going

Tyler, 2026-10-06, in one sitting. Written down because it arrived as one long
thought and half of it would be gone by morning otherwise.

## the thing that was in dispute, settled

Two sessions were pulling opposite ways. One had the shop as The Blind Lead, a
real hunt on public land, every piece carrying a clue. The other was rewriting
the meta toward organic cotton T-shirts from Ridgway. The committed share card
backed the hunt and the uncommitted markup backed the shirts.

**Tyler's ruling: both.** *"The treasure map stuff, the clues on every item, is
correct."* And the products are the products. The shirts are what somebody
buys. The clue is what they find when it arrives. One is the transaction and the
other is the reason, and the shop has been writing as though it had to pick.

So nothing gets removed. The meta rewrite and the hunt live together, and
whoever writes the next headline has to hold both.

## the look

*"The shop should feel clean and good ... a mix of light and dark ... maybe more
of the lime green as like full main colour, on black ... abstract in the
background, heavy on black with lettering and design."*

**Lime gets promoted here and only here.** Across the studio Topo Lime is spent
once per screen. In the shop it is a main colour on black. That is a deliberate
licence and the shop's theme already says the solid button is lime at rest for
the same reason. If anybody reads the house rule and tries to put the lime back
in its box, this paragraph is why they should not.

**Every product image has a transparent background.** That is the whole
opportunity. The product is a cutout and the background behind it can be
anything, so the grid stops being a row of boxes and becomes a set of scenes.

## the backgrounds

Tyler named the references and they are all weather, not ornament:

- Japanese woodblock skies and clouds. The flat banded sky, the carved cloud
- Fluffy. Volume without rendering
- **Komorebi**, sunlight through trees. The Japanese word for exactly that and
  there is no English one

**NO JAPANESE SYMBOLS ON THE SHOP.** His words, and he is right. Characters used
as decoration by people who do not read them is the single most common way this
goes wrong, and it reads as costume to anybody who does. The debt here is to a
way of seeing: flat colour, banded sky, a cloud with an edge, light as a subject
rather than a lighting effect. Borrowing a tradition's eye is ordinary and
respectful. Borrowing its alphabet as pattern is not.

## the language switch

*"At the bottom of the shop it could be like how Phosphor lets you switch into
Japanese ... it translates nice ... let's think of a phrase where Japanese people
won't get disrespected."*

**This one is a promise, and it has to be met before it is made.** A language
toggle says more than "here are the words in your language". It says somebody
here will answer you in it. If a customer in Japan buys a shirt and then the
confirmation mail, the shipping notice, the returns policy and the reply to
their question all arrive in English, the toggle was a costume too, and a worse
one than a symbol on a poster.

So the order is: the storefront words, the transactional mail, the shipping and
returns page, and a human or a burro who can answer. Not the toggle first.

**On the copy itself.** Machine translated marketing copy is where the
disrespect actually happens, not in the fonts. English shop writing leans on
wordplay and a confident register that comes out either comic or faintly rude
when carried over directly. The shop's own voice makes this harder than most:
dry, understated, slightly mysterious.

The way through is not to translate the English. It is to write the Japanese
separately, short, plain and concrete, saying the same true things, and then have
somebody who reads Japanese tell us what it actually sounds like. Short and plain
survives translation. Clever does not.

**The one phrase, when we get there,** should be about the thing, not about us.
What the shop actually is in one line: small runs, made in one town, each piece
carrying a fragment of a real hunt. Every word of that is concrete and none of it
is a pun, which is why it would survive. Nothing is proposed here yet on purpose,
because proposing Japanese copy without a Japanese reader is the mistake this
whole section exists to avoid.

## the order to build it

1. the backgrounds. Scenes behind the cutouts, lime on black, light and dark
2. the lettering and the abstract ground
3. the storefront copy written to survive translation, which improves the
   English too
4. the transactional mail and the policies
5. only then, the switch

No oxford commas, no em dashes.
